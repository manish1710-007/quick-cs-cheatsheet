Foundations of Agile Software Development

What Agile actually is, where it came from, and what it is not

What Agile actually means

Agile is a set of values and principles for software development under uncertainty, published as the Manifesto for Agile Software Development in 2001 by seventeen practitioners (including Kent Beck, Martin Fowler, Robert C. Martin and Ken Schwaber). It is not Scrum, not Kanban, not Jira, and not a ceremony schedule. The Manifesto is one page of values and twelve principles; everything else labeled "Agile" is an interpretation built on top of it — some faithful, some cargo cult.

The core bet of Agile: in most software projects, the biggest risk is building the wrong thing well. Requirements are guesses until real users touch working software. So instead of freezing scope up front and executing a long plan, Agile teams produce working software in short increments, measure the feedback, and adapt both the product and the plan. Agile is how you steer when you cannot fully predict.

Before Agile: the waterfall era

"Waterfall" describes sequential phase-gated development — requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, maintenance — each phase completing and signing off before the next begins. The name comes from a 1970 paper by Winston Royce, which is ironic: Royce was actually describing this model as risky for software and arguing for doing it "twice" and involving the customer — the industry adopted the one-pass version he warned against. Waterfall was formalized in standards like DOD-STD-2167A and remains appropriate where requirements genuinely are stable and verifiable up front (some embedded, safety-critical and hardware-coupled systems).

DimensionWaterfallAgile
RequirementsFixed in a specification phase; changes are change-requested and costlyTreated as hypotheses; a backlog is expected to change and be re-ordered
FeedbackAt the end, when the customer sees the productEvery increment, ideally every sprint or faster
PlanningOne large plan up front; success = conformance to planRolling-wave planning; success = outcomes achieved, plan adapts

Docs & testsHeavy specification documents; testing as a late phaseDocumentation sized to need; testing continuous and automated
Best fitStable, contractually fixed scope; physical constraintsUncertain or evolving scope; software-only systems

Notice what is not in that table: "Waterfall has documents, Agile doesn't" and "Waterfall plans, Agile doesn't." Both are myths. Agile teams plan continuously — release plans, sprint plans, daily plans — and write documentation that has a reader and a purpose. The difference is that plans and documents are treated as tools that change, not as contracts.

Iterative vs incremental — and why Agile is both

The two words are often blurred; they answer different questions:

  • Incremental slices by scope: build a thin piece of the final system, deliver it, add the next piece. A logistics platform ships order creation first, driver assignment second, tracking third. Each increment is a subset of the final product.
  • Iterative refines by revision: build a rough version of the whole thing, get feedback, rework it closer to right. The first tracking page is a crude map ping; after feedback it gains ETA prediction, geofencing and proof-of-delivery photos.

Agile combines both: a sprint produces an increment of working software, and the backlog is re-ordered and refined each cycle based on what the increment taught you. Waterfall is neither — it is a single long pass.

The four values

#Manifesto statementWhat it means in an engineering team
1Individuals and interactions over processes and toolsA CI pipeline nobody looks at helps no one; conversation resolves in minutes what a ticket war resolves in weeks. But the value has an "over," not an "instead" — tools still matter.
2Working software over comprehensive documentationThe demo of a deployed order API beats a 90-page interface spec for validating direction. Documents that a real reader needs are still written.
3Customer collaboration over contract negotiationProduct Owners talk to dispatch operators weekly, not only at sign-off. Scope conversations are joint problem-solving, not change-order litigation.
4Responding to change over following a planWhen a competitor launches live tracking, the backlog re-orders next refinement — the plan is a forecast, not a vow.

Every line ends with "while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more." Teams that read only the left half and delete the right half are not Agile; they are chaotic.

The twelve principles — grouped for working memory

  • Delivery rhythm: (1) highest priority is early and continuous delivery of valuable software; (2) welcome changing requirements, even late — change is competitive advantage; (3) deliver working software frequently, from weeks to months, preferring the shorter timescale.
  • People: (4) business and developers must work together daily; (5) build projects around motivated individuals, give them the environment and trust; (6) face-to-face conversation is the best form of communication (a remote team translates this to video and async writing).
  • Quality as the enabler: (7) working software is the primary measure of progress; (8) sustainable pace — able to maintain a constant pace indefinitely; (9) continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility.
  • Simplicity and self-organization: (10) simplicity — the art of maximizing work not done; (11) the best architectures, requirements and designs emerge from self-organizing teams.
  • Improvement loop: (12) at regular intervals the team reflects and adjusts its behavior.

Principles 7 and 9 are the engineer's favorites: "working software" means tested, deployable software, and "technical excellence" is what keeps the fifth increment as cheap as the first. Without them, agility decays into shipping garbage faster.

Empirical process control: the engine under everything

Agile treats software development as an empirical process — one where the outcome is only knowable by observing it, not by deriving it in advance. Empirical control stands on three pillars, which Scrum adopted directly:

  • Transparency — the real state of work and quality is visible to everyone doing the work: an honest backlog, a board that reflects reality, CI dashboards anyone can read, "done" that means done.
  • Inspection — frequent, deliberate examination of that reality: review the increment, inspect the flow, read the cycle-time data, demo to users.
  • Adaptation — when inspection reveals a gap, adjust the process, plan or product soon — next sprint, next refinement, today's plan.

Contrast with defined process control: if development were truly repeatable like an assembly line, you could specify the perfect process once and run it. Fifty years of schedule overruns say otherwise. The feedback loop — build, measure, learn — is not overhead; it is the mechanism.

build increment→inspect (demo, metrics, users)→adapt (backlog, process)→repeat faster
❖Scenario — why feedback matters
A logistics team spends four waterfall months building a driver mobile app exactly to the signed specification. At UAT, dispatchers reveal the operational truth: drivers lose connectivity in freight elevators and tunnels, so the app must support offline job capture with conflict resolution on reconnect. The spec was detailed, reviewed and wrong — because nobody ran real drivers through real buildings. An Agile counterpart would have put a walking-skeleton app in a driver's hands in week two and learned this for the price of one increment. That is the entire business case for short feedback loops.

Mindset vs ceremonies

A team can run every ceremony on schedule and still be waterfall with stand-ups: work thrown over walls, testers as a late phase, scope locked by a quarterly commitment. Conversely, a team with no formal framework can be deeply Agile: shipping weekly, measuring flow, talking to users, adapting. The ceremonies are sensors and actuators; the mindset is the control system. When the ceremony stops producing inspection and adaptation, it has become theater — Module 21 catalogs the full zoo.

!Common misconceptions
  • "Agile means no planning." Agile teams plan more often than waterfall teams — release forecasting, sprint planning, daily re-planning. What changes is the horizon of commitment: detailed near-term, coarse far-term.
  • "Agile means no documentation." The Manifesto values working software over comprehensive documentation — and says the right has value. An API without reference docs is not "Agile," it is unusable.
  • "Agile is a speed/cheapness method." It is an uncertainty-management method. Teams often deliver faster because they stop building the wrong half of the spec, not because typing got quicker.
  • "Agile = Scrum." Scrum is one framework within Agile. Kanban, XP and Lean are others; Module 02 compares them.
  • "We do Agile, so process is whatever each dev decides." Self-organization without shared engineering discipline is chaos with a board. XP exists precisely because process rigor and agility are allies.

Adapting requirements during development — the normal case

Mid-project, the logistics platform's biggest customer announces a merger: orders will now originate from two ERPs with different identifiers. A waterfall team negotiates a change request and re-baselines. An Agile team: the Product Owner splits the order intake epic, adds a story for a second inbound adapter behind a feature flag, demotes the single-ERP reporting story, and raises a spike to resolve identifier mapping. Velocity dips one sprint (learning + refactor); the roadmap survives. The backlog absorbing the change is the design working as intended.

iInterview lens — Module 01
Expect: "What is Agile?" (values + principles + empiricism, not ceremonies), "Agile vs Waterfall" (risk profile and feedback timing, not old vs new), "Does Agile mean no documentation/planning?" (no — rolling-wave planning, docs with a reader), and "What is empirical process control?" (transparency, inspection, adaptation). Strong answers cite why: uncertainty makes big-batch validation the dominant risk.

Agile Frameworks and Methodologies

Scrum, Kanban, XP, Lean, FDD, Crystal, SAFe, Scrumban — and how to choose honestly

Frameworks are reusable process designs that implement Agile values. Each one makes different assumptions about the shape of your work and the size of your organization. Knowing them lets you borrow deliberately instead of adopting labels. And remember the anti-dogma rule: not every organization needs a named framework at all — a small, stable, collocated team with good engineering habits may need only a board, a cadence and a retro.

Team size and organizational fit

  • 1–9 people, one team: anything works; Scrum or Kanban or plain XP habits. Focus on engineering quality and a visible board.
  • 10–50 people, 2–6 teams, one product: Scrum of Scrums, Nexus or LeSS if dependencies are dense; Kanban at the program layer helps intake.
  • 50–500+, portfolio pressure: SAFe or DA-style scaled approaches enter — and so do their costs. Module 20 covers the trade-offs in detail.
  • Production support / ops-heavy teams: Kanban (Module 14) almost always fits better than sprint commitments, because arrival is unpredictable.

A framework selection decision guide

unpredictable intake?→Kanban|product discovery, stable team?→Scrum|quality on fire?→add XP
  1. Start from the work, not the framework. Write down the last 200 items your team actually did. Feature work? Incidents? Support? Both? The mix tells you the shape.
  2. Predictability of arrival. Mostly planned features → sprint cadence works. >30% unplanned → flow-based (Kanban/Scrumban) before you blame "failed sprints."
  3. Feedback need. Who must see progress, how often, and can they actually attend? If stakeholders cannot engage per-sprint, cadence value drops.
  4. Engineering maturity. Weak CI, no tests, long-lived branches: framework choice is secondary — fix Module 17 first, or every framework disappoints.
  5. Coordination surface. Count cross-team dependencies per feature. High density → invest in architecture decoupling and multi-team events (Module 20), not just process.
  6. Regulatory reality. Validated industries may need documentation gates; choose hybrids consciously and keep feedback loops inside them.
!Do not adopt a framework to solve a non-process problem
Slow delivery caused by a 4-hour integration test suite will not respond to SAFe. A toxic blame culture will defeat the best retro format. Misassigned frameworks are expensive camouflage: they consume change energy while the real constraint (architecture, staffing, incentives, environment) stays untouched. Diagnose the constraint first.
❖Practice this decision
Use the Agile Framework Selector workshop: answer seven questions about a team's work shape and get a reasoned Scrum / Kanban / Scrumban / hybrid recommendation with its limits spelled out. Then compare with what the team above actually chose and why.
iInterview lens — Module 02
"Compare Scrum and Kanban" (cadence vs flow — Module 16 has the full table), "What is XP and why do engineers love it?" (engineering practices as agility's foundation), "When would you NOT use Scrum?" (unpredictable intake, pure maintenance/support), "What is Scrumban?" (transition hybrid), "Is SAFe Agile?" — the honest answer: it scales Agile concepts to enterprise constraints, with real costs; judge it by outcomes, not purity.

Scrum Fundamentals

The framework exactly as the Scrum Guide defines it — theory, values, pillars, accountabilities, artifacts

Scrum is a lightweight framework for developing complex products, defined by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland in the Scrum Guide, revised several times with the 2020 edition the current baseline. The Guide is deliberately short — about 13 pages — and precise about what is Scrum and what is "common practice." This module follows its terminology; anything the Guide does not mandate is labeled practice in this guide.

What the Guide actually says Scrum is

Scrum is a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems.

Three things make it work: empiricism (knowledge comes from experience and decisions on observations — Module 01's three pillars), lean thinking (reduce waste, focus on essentials, deliver in small batches), and small self-managing teams. Scrum is intentionally incomplete: it tells you the events, accountabilities and artifacts, and expects you to add the specific tactics — test strategy, tooling, engineering practice — within its boundaries.

Scrum theory and the five values

Scrum's pillars are Transparency, Inspection, Adaptation. The 2020 Guide lists five values that make the pillars real in daily behavior:

ValueDaily behavior that proves itBehavior that falsifies it
CommitmentThe team is personally invested in the Sprint Goal and quality bar"Management committed us to 60 points" — commitment assigned, not owned
FocusConcentrate on the Sprint Goal and the work of the sprintSix half-finished stories, four parallel side-projects, "quick favors" all sprint
OpennessThe team and stakeholders are open about the work and the challengesGreen board, red reality; blockers mentioned only at sprint end
RespectTeam members respect each other as capable, independent peopleEstimates shouted down by seniority; review theater where nobody speaks truth
CourageDo the right thing and work on tough problems — say "no," show real status, refactor the scary partEvery sprint over-committed silently; nobody tells the PO the story is impossible

The values are not decoration: empirical process control fails without them. If status is not open (transparency), inspection sees fiction; if nobody has courage to adapt (drop scope, change plan), inspection is wasted.

In earlier Guides these were "goals" loosely; the 2020 text formalized the triad.

iWhy "accountabilities" matters in an interview
Saying "Scrum has three roles" dates you to 2017. The 2020 reframe answers a real confusion: a person can hold an accountability without it being their whole job, and no committee can dilute the Product Owner's backlog authority. If your interviewer still says "roles," gently align: "the current Guide calls them accountabilities — same three."

The Guide's own words: Scrum is "a framework within which people can address complex adaptive problems." If your additions reduce transparency or focus, they are anti-Scrum regardless of intent.

Scrum Accountabilities and Team Structure

Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers — and everyone around them

Product Owner — maximize the value of the product

The PO is a single person accountable for product value and for Product Backlog management: developing and communicating the Product Goal, creating and ordering backlog items, and ensuring transparency and understanding of the backlog. One person, not a committee — committees may advise, but one human owns the ordering decision. The organization must respect the PO's decisions as visible in the backlog: if anyone can override ordering by escalation, you have a committee with extra steps.

  • Product vision — the story of who the product serves and how it wins; makes ordering decisions explainable ("live tracking first, because churn is concentrated in the first 30 days").
  • Backlog ordering — a continuous value/risk/cost/learning sort, not a one-time prioritization. Module 06 covers methods (MoSCoW, RICE, WSJF) as decision aids.
  • Stakeholder collaboration — translating operators, sales, compliance and executives into one ordered list, and translating the backlog back into "why."
  • Decision authority — final call on what is in the sprint's candidate set; not authority over how Developers build it.
  • Clarification — reachable during the sprint for acceptance and scope questions; a PO who answers next Tuesday makes every story slower.
!The "ticket administrator" failure
A PO who only types stakeholder requests into Jira and asks "is it done yet?" has outsourced value maximization to whoever shouts loudest. The PO's craft is saying no (or "not yet") with a reason, and saying yes to the few things that move the Product Goal. Module 21 dissects this anti-pattern.

Scrum Master — establish Scrum's effectiveness

The 2020 Guide tightened the Scrum Master accountability to the effectiveness of Scrum: coaching self-management, helping everyone understand Scrum theory and practice, and removing impediments by causing change that increases the team's productivity. The Scrum Master serves the team, the PO and the organization — and is not a project manager, task assigner, or meeting secretary, though bad organizations force them into exactly that shape.

Scrum Master doesScrum Master is not
  • Coaches the Daily Scrum as the Developers' own planning session (and often stops attending once it works)
  • Facilitates events when facilitation adds value — and teaches others to facilitate
  • Works on impediments systemically: the flaky test environment is fixed once, for everyone
  • Helps the PO with backlog techniques, goal definition and empirical forecasting
  • Coaches the organization through Scrum adoption: expectations, incentives, interfaces
  • Protects focus: WIP conversations, interruption policies, definition of "done" adoption
  • A task manager assigning work to developers
  • A status reporter upward ("the Scrum Master tracks velocity for management")
  • A meeting scheduler whose calendar is the job
  • A delivery commitment negotiator (that's the whole Scrum Team's conversation)
  • A blocker of business contact with the team — transparency means stakeholders can see work; the SM channels it, not bans it

Developers — create the Increment

Developers are everyone committed to building any aspect of the increment each sprint — backend, frontend, QA, UX, DBA, SRE as the product needs. Their accountabilities per the Guide:

  • Create and own the Sprint Backlog — select items (with the PO), decompose to tasks, own the plan toward the Sprint Goal.
  • Uphold quality — adhere to the Definition of Done; adapt it only with discipline (never mid-sprint to rescue scope).
  • Adapt daily — replan each day toward the Sprint Goal; escalate early when the goal is at risk.
  • Hold each other professionally accountable — code review, pairing, shared standards, no "not my module."

Cross-functional and self-managing

  • Cross-functional = the team has every competency needed to turn a backlog item into a Done increment without external handoffs. A team that must wait for a separate QA phase or an external DBA approval is not cross-functional — it has a queue.
  • Self-managing = the team decides who, how and what-order within the sprint (the "what" is negotiated with the PO; the "why" is the Sprint Goal). It does not mean self-directing the product.

❖Responsibility boundaries — a worked example
Logistics platform, sprint 7. The PO orders "assign driver automatically" above "manual batch assignment" (churn data says delays drive cancellations). Developers estimate both, warn the auto-assign story needs a routing algorithm spike, and propose the spike first. The Scrum Master notices QA was absent from refinement and facilitates a session so test perspective enters acceptance criteria before planning. The stakeholder (head of dispatch) wants manual batch "because a big customer asked"; the PO negotiates: batch enters the backlog at position 4, behind the churn driver. Nobody assigned tasks to anybody; three accountabilities did three different jobs on one decision.

Team structure misunderstandings

  • "Scrum Master = team lead." No — the team leads itself; the SM improves the system within which it does.
  • "PO = product manager with more meetings." A PM manages markets and roadmaps; the PO additionally owns the ordered backlog and works with the team daily. In large orgs they may be different people — the PO must stay close to the build.
  • "Developers = programmers only." Anyone building the increment. If your QA "tests the increment," your QA is a Developer.
  • "Self-managing = no managers." Managers still manage people and org design; they simply stop directing daily engineering work.

Scrum Events and Ceremonies

Planning, Daily, Review, Retrospective — purpose, outcomes, timeboxes, facilitation, failures

Scrum's events are fixed containers for inspection and adaptation — each one inspects something specific and produces a specific adaptation. Treat the timeboxes as maximums: a sharp Daily Scrum ends in 9 minutes; a good retro uses its full budget. For a one-month sprint the Guide caps Planning at 8h, Daily at 15min, Review at 4h, Retro at 3h — shorter sprints take proportionally shorter events (a 2-week sprint commonly plans in 2–4 hours).

1. Sprint Planning — why is this sprint valuable and how will we do it?

  • Participants: whole Scrum Team; PO brings the ordered backlog and Product Goal context; stakeholders only if the team invites them for specific questions.
  • Preparation: top backlog items refined to Ready (Module 08); capacity known; last sprint's velocity and carryover known.
  • Topics (Guide structure): (1) Why is this Sprint valuable? → Sprint Goal. (2) What can be Done this Sprint? → selection. (3) How will the chosen work get done? → tasks and plan.
  • Outcome: a Sprint Backlog — Sprint Goal + selected items + the Developers' plan of tasks.
  • Common mistakes: selecting by velocity arithmetic with no goal; PO pre-assigning the scope; planning tasks to hours for 10 days (false precision); no capacity math; ignoring dependencies until day 3.
  • Facilitation: timebox the "why" (10 min), draft the goal before selecting items, select, decompose only the first 2–3 days in detail, park unknowns as sprint-internal spikes.

2. Daily Scrum — inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal

  • Participants: Developers (it is their event). PO/SM attend only if they actively add value as Developers invite.
  • Outcome: a re-planned day — new information, blockers surfaced, help arranged, swarm decisions.
  • The three questions are tradition, not mandate: the 2020 Guide dropped them. What is mandatory is the inspect-and-adapt purpose. Alternatives: walk the board right-to-left, "what's blocking the Sprint Goal?", 5-minute board walk + 10-minute breakout.
  • Common mistakes: status report to a manager; problem-solving the whole 15 minutes (take it offline); absent members; skipping when busy — exactly when it's most needed.

Module 13 gives the Daily Scrum a full module with effective/ineffective transcripts.

3. Sprint Review — inspect the increment, adapt the backlog

  • Participants: Scrum Team + stakeholders the PO invites (users, operators, sales, executives as useful).
  • Preparation: the increment genuinely Done and demonstrable in a realistic environment; PO pre-shares what will be shown; stakeholders who can actually decide attend.
  • Outcome: next-step backlog adjustments from real conversation — the review is the feedback event, not a ceremony to survive.
  • Common mistakes: slide decks instead of software; demos of half-done work labeled done; stakeholders who watch silently and escalate after; skipping review in crunch sprints (removing the steering wheel while speeding).
  • Facilitation: start from the Sprint Goal ("did we get what we aimed for?"), demonstrate the increment hands-on, then openly discuss market/usage data, budget/timeline, and next most valuable things. Capture backlog changes visibly.

4. Sprint Retrospective — inspect the process, plan improvement

  • Participants: Scrum Team only — psychological safety is the point; managers attending changes the speech acts.
  • Outcome: one to three concrete improvement actions with owners — ideally inserted into the next Sprint Backlog so improvement gets capacity like any other work.
  • Common mistakes: venting with no actions; actions with no owner; same "communication could be better" every month; SM presenting a solution the team never chose.
  • Formats: Start/Stop/Continue, 4Ls (Liked, Learned, Lacked, Longed for), Sailboat, Mad/Sad/Glad, timeline replay for incident-heavy sprints. Rotate to fight format fatigue.
❖Practice retrospectives
Run a working Retrospective Workshop board (Start/Stop/Continue or 4Ls), vote on themes, and convert them into specific actions — then test your actions against the Retro Action Evaluator to see whether "improve code reviews" became something a human can actually execute next sprint.

Backlog refinement — the ongoing non-event

Refinement (the 2020 Guide's term; older texts say "grooming") is the continuous activity of adding detail, order and size to backlog items: splitting epics, writing acceptance criteria, estimating, killing zombie stories. It is not one of the five formal events, has no Guide timebox, and commonly consumes up to ~10% of a sprint's capacity as one or two sessions. Skipping refinement starves the next Planning: stories arrive vague, and planning degrades into a requirements workshop with extra pressure.

Sprint cancellation — the rare, valid kill switch

Only the Product Owner may cancel a sprint, and the Guide expects it rarely — when the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete: the merger invalidates the feature, regulation kills the market, a pivot makes the goal pointless. Completed work meeting DoD is reviewed; the rest returns to the Product Backlog; a new Planning starts a fresh sprint. A sprint is not cancelled because the team is behind — that's a scope conversation at Daily Scrum or mid-sprint negotiation with the PO.

Remote and hybrid ceremonies

  • Cameras on, one voice at a time, round-robin cold calls beat "anyone? ...anyone?" silences.
  • Digital boards are the shared brain: everyone moves cards in real time; the person speaking shares screen only for demos.
  • Async-first Daily Scrum (written update in channel + optional 10-min blocker call) works for spread time zones — keep the "inspect toward Sprint Goal" purpose in text, not a bureaucratic form.
  • Retros run better with silent writing first (shared doc, 5 minutes) so introverts and non-native speakers enter the discussion with their ideas already visible.
!Extra meetings are practice, not Scrum
Teams commonly add: refinement sessions (fine), a weekly "Tech Sync" (fine), a mid-sprint scope check with the PO (fine — Scrum allows scope negotiation), and a daily "Scrum of Scrums" in multi-team setups (Module 20). The test for each: does it produce inspection-and-adaptation that an existing event cannot? If a meeting exists because "we always had it," audit it.
iInterview lens — Module 05
Know the five events with purposes, participants and timebox maximums (8h/15m/4h/3h per one-month sprint, scaling down for shorter sprints). Expect "what if a story is incomplete at sprint end?" (returns to backlog — nothing rolls over as a habit; re-estimate from current understanding), "who may cancel a sprint and when?" (PO, obsolete goal), "must the Daily Scrum use three questions?" (no — the Guide dropped them; the inspect/adapt purpose is what counts).

The Product Backlog is the single, ordered, evolving source of everything the product needs — features, bugs, technical work, research. "Single" is load-bearing: parallel lists (a separate "tech backlog" nobody orders, a "QA backlog") are where priority inversion hides. The 2020 Guide adds the Product Goal as the backlog's commitment: a longer-term objective that gives ordering a north star and makes "why is this first?" answerable.

The Guide says the backlog is ordered, a strict sequence (1st, 2nd, 3rd...) — not buckets of High/Medium/Low where fifty things are all "High." Refinement: keeping the top alive

  • Cadence: one or two sessions per sprint, ~10% capacity, Developers + PO (+ QA voice — testability enters cheapest here).

  • Depth gradient: top 2 sprints sprint-ready, next 2 estimated and coarse, the rest epic placeholders. A fully-detailed year of backlog is waste (detail decays).
  • Managing uncertainty: uncertainty > a sprint and load-bearing? Insert a spike and order it before the dependent stories. That's refinement doing risk management.
❖Backlog health checks
  • Ready depth: ≥ 1.5–2 sprints of Ready items exists (no starvation of Planning).
  • Staleness: items older than ~3 months with no touch and no explanation — delete or consciously park with a revisit date.
  • Size mix: most stories carry to Done in ≤ half a sprint; the "big rock" ratio shrinking each month.
  • Transparency: anyone on the team can explain any top-10 item's value without asking.
  • Debt visibility: technical-debt work occupies a visible slice of the order, not a hidden side-list.

User Stories and Acceptance Criteria

INVEST, personas, Given-When-Then, BDD, edge cases — with ten worked examples

A user story is a promise for a conversation (Ron Jeffries' "3 Cs": Card, Conversation, Confirmation). The card carries just enough intent to schedule the conversation; the acceptance criteria confirm the outcome. A story is not a technical task wearing a template — "As a developer I want to refactor the DAO layer so that code is cleaner" has no user value to negotiate; it is honest work that belongs on the backlog as a tech item, not a story in costume.

Format and intent

As a <role>, I want <capability>, so that <business value>.

The three slots exist to force three different questions: who (which persona — not "user" of 40,000 people), what (capability, not UI detail), why (the value that lets a PO trade scope intelligently). When scope must shrink, the "so that" tells you which thinner slice still delivers it.

Personas and needs

Stories sharpen when the "who" is real: Dispatcher Dana (juggles 40 live orders, dual monitors, keyboard-first), Driver Dev (on the road, flaky 4G, one hand on the wheel — his app must survive elevators), Shipper Sam (the paying customer, cares about price and proof), Accounting Ada (needs clean invoicing truth). A persona is a compression of research, not a fictional friend: it encodes constraints ("Dev loses connectivity") that become non-functional requirements and edge cases.

Acceptance criteria and Given-When-Then

Acceptance criteria (AC) are the conditions of satisfaction: observable, testable statements agreed before build. The Given-When-Then form (from BDD, via Dan North) gives them structure:

Given <initial context> When <event/action> Then <observable outcome>

Behavior-Driven Development turns AC into executable specifications (Cucumber/JBehave/Gauge): the Gherkin file runs as an automated test against the real system. BDD's real value is the conversation between PO, developer and tester before implementation — "three amigos" — not the tooling. A Cucumber suite nobody reads is just slow unit testing with extra syntax.

Functional vs non-functional, edge and negative cases

  • Functional: "Then the order status changes to ASSIGNED and Dev receives the job." What the system does.
  • Non-functional: "p95 status refresh < 2s on 3G"; "password reset tokens expire in 30 minutes"; "API returns 429 after 100 req/min per key." How well it does it — often constraint-style AC or global DoD items.
  • Negative/edge scenarios belong in the story: wrong credentials, expired token, elevator-offline driver, double-click submit, zero results, unicode address, midnight/timezone boundaries. A story whose AC has only the happy path is half a story — the cheap half.

Definition of Ready and Definition of Done

Two quality contracts — one official, one a team practice

Definition of Done is Scrum Guide canon: the formal description of the state the Increment must meet — quality bars the Developers commit to for every item. Definition of Ready is not: it is a widespread team practice (a pre-conditions checklist for "this story can enter a sprint") that the Guide neither requires nor forbids. Say that sentence in an interview and you separate yourself from 90% of candidates who present DoR and DoD as twins.

Why DoD exists

Without a shared DoD, "done" means whatever the last person to touch the story means — "code complete" (untested), "tested" (not deployed), "deployed" (not documented). Each gap is undone work with interest that someone pays later, invisibly. The DoD turns "done" into a binary, auditable contract: an increment is either fully done or not started being done. The Guide's chain: a Sprint Review inspects an Increment; an Increment is only an Increment if it meets DoD.

Definition of Done — example checklist (backend team)

Code & design
  • Code reviewed by a second developer; discussion resolved
  • Follows team coding standards (formatter + static analysis clean)
  • No new compiler/linter warnings
  • Public API changes reflected in OpenAPI spec
Tests
  • Unit tests for new/changed logic; failure paths covered
  • Integration tests for DB/API boundaries
  • All tests green in CI on the PR
  • No test skipped without a linked issue
Security & data
  • Secrets in vault/env, never in code or logs
  • Input validation on trust boundaries
  • Migrations forward-only, reviewed, tested on prod-like data
  • No PII in logs; audit events where required
Ops & release
  • Deployed to staging via pipeline; smoke test passes
  • Feature flagged when behavior is risky
  • Observability: metrics/logs/traces for new paths
  • Runbook/changelog updated when behavior changes

Definition of Ready — example checklist (team practice)

  • Story states role, capability, value (not solution details)
  • Acceptance criteria written, testable, covering negative paths
  • Dependencies identified and either resolved or explicitly accepted
  • Estimated by the team; unknowns converted to spikes
  • UX assets (when needed) available — not "coming Thursday"
  • No open question older than one refinement without an owner
!DoR's failure mode: the waterfall toll booth
A DoR becomes toxic when it hardens into a bureaucratic gate ("no story enters a sprint without 100% of 18 fields") — that is mini-waterfall with Agile stationery. A healthy DoR is a conversation aid: it tells refinement what to solve. Also: DoR is not a guarantee, and a story can honestly enter a sprint with known unknowns if the team consciously reserves discovery time. Never use DoR to blame the PO ("not ready, rejected") — use it to make the next refinement better.

  • Debt as backlog items with their own value story ("unblocks auto-assign scale target"), ordered by consequence, sized honestly.

  • Deliberate debt: sometimes you consciously ship a shortcut to hit a window — that's fine if the debt is written down, visible on the board with a cost note, and ordered like any other work. Undocumented shortcuts aren't decisions; they're surprises.

Agile Estimation Fundamentals

Why estimating software is hard, what estimates actually are, and how bias corrupts them

Why software estimation is difficult

Software estimation fails for structural reasons, not because estimators are lazy. First, the thing being estimated does not exist yet — you are estimating a discovery process. Second, work arrives with irreducible uncertainty: the unknowns are unknown until you touch the code, the data, the integration. Third, software is designed, not manufactured: the first unit costs enormously more than the millionth, and novel vs repeated work mixes in every backlog. Fourth, humans estimate in favorable imagined worlds (no meetings, no flaky tests, no production incident) — the planning fallacy, documented across domains since Kahneman and Tversky.

iThe cone of uncertainty
Early in a project, honest estimates vary by 4× in either direction; the band narrows as decisions settle and code exists. This is why Agile teams estimate in ranges, re-estimate with fresh information, and refuse single-number dates for distant work. A sprint-24 date given in sprint 4 is not a forecast; it is a wish with a calendar reference.

Effort vs duration; estimate vs commitment

  • Effort is work content (person-hours of building). Duration is calendar time, inflated by availability (meetings, support, focus fragmentation) and by dependencies and queues. A 16-effort-hour story is not "two days" for someone with 40% meeting load and a code-review queue.
  • An estimate is a forecast with a probability distribution — "about 8 points, could be 5, could be 13 if the migration bites." A commitment is a promise to try; a target is a business wish. When management converts estimates into commitments and commitments into performance reviews, estimators respond rationally: they pad, sandbag, and stop being honest. The information channel dies precisely when it's most needed.

Relative vs absolute estimation

Humans are bad at absolute judgment ("this takes 27 hours") and comparatively good at relative judgment ("this is bigger than that, similar to that other one"). Story points exploit this: an arbitrary unit that stays stable within a team, calibrated against reference stories. Absolute time units invite the two classic failures: converting directly to calendar promises, and comparing people's "hours" as productivity. Points resist both by design — they are team-relative, not clock-convertible.

Estimation at different levels

  • Release/quarter: t-shirt or very coarse points; output is a range and a confidence, not a date to the day.
  • Sprint/story: relative points calibrated by the team that builds them.
  • Task (inside the sprint): hours are fine — short horizon, the estimators are the executors, and yesterday's evidence corrects today.
  • Refinement-level sizing (a quick relative sort) vs formal estimation (poker rounds with discussion): don't spend ceremony on items that aren't about to be built.

Estimates as forecasts — using history

Mature teams forecast from throughput data: "we finish 28–36 points per sprint with 85% of sprints between 24 and 38; 7 sprints of data." A forecast states a range and a confidence ("80% likely by sprint 14"). This replaces the sad ritual of averaging story-level wishes into a precise-looking wrong date. Confidence levels also discipline scope: raise confidence by cutting scope, not by arguing.

!Never rank developers by points
Story points are a team forecasting signal, not a productivity metric. Points-per-developer comparisons are invalid for four independent reasons: (1) points are relative to each team's calibration — no shared zero; (2) points measure size of items taken, not value or difficulty of solving; (3) the moment points touch performance reviews, inflation begins and the signal dies (Goodhart's Law — Module 18); (4) it destroys the collaboration estimation needs — nobody helps a teammate size down a story if helping costs their "score." If an organization insists, honest engineers should refuse the practice and teach why; this is a hill worth dying on.

Team-based estimation via simultaneous reveal, popularized by James Grenning and Mike Cohn. The ritual is engineered to defeat Module 09's biases: simultaneous cards prevent anchoring; discussion of outliers surfaces hidden knowledge; the group owns the number.

  1. Moderator (often SM, rotatable) reads the story; the PO (or author) answers clarifying questions — no estimating yet.
  2. Everyone selects a card in silence: 0, ½, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, 100, ? (unestimable), ∞ (needs splitting now).
  3. Reveal simultaneously. All at once — that simultaneity is the mechanism, not theater.
  4. Outliers argue first: highest and lowest explain their reasoning. The low estimator knows a shortcut; the high estimator sees a trap. Both inform the room.
  5. Re-vote after discussion. Convergence in 2–3 rounds is normal; a second identical split means the story is genuinely ambiguous — split it or spike it rather than averaging to a fake consensus number.
  6. Record the consensus with its doubts ("8, worried about the migration lock" — that doubt is sprint-planning information).
  • Remote variant: same flow over video with a planning-poker app or chat-emoji reveals; keep the simultaneous-reveal rule sacred.
  • Strengths: cheap bias defense, knowledge sharing disguised as estimation, fast (2–4 min per story once warmed).
  • Weaknesses: slower with 10+ people (split the room); can fatigue on 30-story backlogs (use affinity first, poker the contentious); social pressure survives if seniors dominate discussion even without cards.

Why Fibonacci (and the 1–100 ladder)

The modified ladder 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, 100 encodes the cone of uncertainty: gaps widen as size grows, making precision honest — nobody can pretend a 13 and a 14 are meaningfully different, but 5-vs-8 is a real decision. 20/40/100 are effectively "big, bigger, enormous — split before planning." Original Fibonacci also satisfies the math of error propagation: when summed, errors don't create false precision.

T-shirt sizing

XS/S/M/L/XL for coarse relative buckets — roadmap and epic territory, portfolio Kanban, or a young team calibrating. Strength: fast, zero fake precision, non-threatening (no numbers to weaponize). Weakness: can't be summed into velocity; eventually epics must decompose into estimable stories. Mapping M→"about half a sprint" only within a team's own calibration.

Affinity estimation

For a large backlog: write every item on a card, then as a group place them on a relative line (smallest left, largest right), discussing only disagreements ("why is driver-rating near tiny?"). Refine into columns, assign the column's number. Suits 50–300 items in an hour; brilliant kick-off for a new product; weak when items are abstract or team knowledge is uneven — placement debates just move vocabulary.

Bucket system

Speed variant of affinity: fix the buckets (the Fibonacci ladder), pass items one by one, each gets dropped into a bucket with a timebox per item; anyone can challenge by moving it later. Handles hundreds of items in one session. Limitation: single-item attention is seconds, so noisy placements need a cleanup pass — treat output as a draft, not a contract.

Three-point estimation and PERT

  • O — optimistic (everything goes right), M — most likely, P — pessimistic (reasonable bad luck, not catastrophe).
Expected estimate E = (O + 4M + P) / 6
❖Worked example
"Add carrier webhooks": O = 3d (contract stable, sandbox works), M = 6d, P = 14d (sandbox auth + their staging is down half the time). E = (3 + 24 + 14)/6 = 6.83 ≈ 7 days. Assumptions to state aloud: the beta-distribution behind the weights is an approximation; P excludes black swans ("they never deliver an API"); and the output is a mean, not a promise — for a delivery buffer use the spread (P−O = 11d says: this item carries real risk).

NoEstimates

The movement (its most visible voice: Woody Zuill) argues teams should stop estimating stories and forecast from flow data instead: split work into small similarly-sized slices, track throughput (items per week), and forecast by counting ("~14 items/week → 42-item scope ≈ 3 weeks, 85th percentile from history").

  • When it works: mature slicing discipline (stories genuinely small and similar), stable team, Kanban-style flow, a stakeholder who accepts probabilistic answers.
  • Limitations: it doesn't remove estimation — it moves it into slicing (bad slicing = broken forecasts); "how long?" still needs a scope count; large items corrupt item-count math.
  • Misunderstandings to avoid: NoEstimates is not "no planning," and not an excuse to skip thinking; it is a bet that flow history beats per-item judgment, given the preconditions.

Velocity, Capacity, and Forecasting

What velocity can honestly tell you — and everything it cannot

The formulas (definitions first)

Velocity = sum of story points completed (to Done) in one sprint
Average Velocity = total completed points / number of sprints
Sprint Capacity = the team's genuinely available working time this sprint

Velocity is a team-level, retrospective, forecasting signal: how much Done work this team absorbs per sprint under recent real conditions. Capacity is a forward-looking input: how much presence and attention the team actually has this sprint (holidays, leave, on-call, workshops). They are different quantities in different units — story points and hours are not interchangeable, and "capacity" is not "velocity converted to hours." Velocity tells you what a normal sprint delivers; capacity tells you this sprint has 40% of the team at a conference.

What velocity is NOT

  • Not a productivity score. Nothing in the unit measures output value. A team whose velocity rises from 30 to 45 may have improved — or re-calibrated points, split stories finer, absorbed scope silently, or been handed easier work.
  • Not comparable across teams. A 5 in team A's calibration is not a 5 in team B's. Cross-team velocity leagues create instant point inflation (Module 21).
  • Not a target or quota. The moment velocity becomes a goal, it stops measuring anything (Goodhart). A healthy velocity chart wobbles — 31, 27, 34, 29 — because reality wobbles.
  • Not a performance metric for individuals — see Module 09's four independent reasons.

Capacity planning — the honest arithmetic

  1. Gross time: 6 developers × 10 working days = 60 person-days.
  2. Subtract absence: −1 on jury duty (5), −2 vacation (10) → 45 person-days.
  3. Subtract standing load: daily scrums/planning/review/retro/refinement ≈ 12% (−5.4); on-call rotation (−3); production support rota this sprint (−4) → ~32.6.
  4. Subtract known interruptions: quarterly release window prep (−3) → ~29.6 person-days.
  5. Convert to expectation: compare against what a full-capacity sprint delivered (historical velocity was measured at ~full team) → plan a point budget proportionally below normal velocity, or explicitly reserve capacity buckets.

The focus factor (planned points / theoretical capacity) is a rough sanity ratio with real limits: it mixes points with time in one fraction, so treat it as a trend signal for the same team, never a cross-team number or a target ("all teams must be 70%") — that's Goodhart bait.

Forecasting a release

Sprints needed ≈ remaining points / average velocity — then widen: quote the range

Remaining scope 180 points, average 29, range 24–35 → 6.2 sprints average, but the honest statement is "5.1–7.5 sprints; commit externally to the 75–85th percentile (about 7–8), and improve the number by cutting scope, not by hoping." Monte Carlo tools (or a spreadsheet with resampled sprint history) do this properly; the discipline matters more than the tool: ranges with confidence, reviewed every sprint, scope as the steering variable.

!"Velocity went up, so the team improved" — maybe not
Before celebrating a velocity rise, check: point calibration drift (old 5s would now be 8s), story granularity (more, smaller stories = more points for equal work), definition-of-done erosion (less testing per point), and mix change (easy bug-fix sprint). The only clean productivity evidence is throughput of valuable outcomes at constant quality — DORA metrics (Module 18) plus product outcomes, not velocity slope.

Daily Scrum and Sprint Execution Practices

Fifteen minutes of steering — not a status ritual

The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their day. It is planning, not reporting: the audience is each other, the output is a re-planned day. The 2020 Guide explicitly dropped the "three questions" as a requirement — they remain one workable format, nothing more.

Traditional format — and why it often decays

"What did I do yesterday? What will I do today? Any impediment?" Decay mode: each answer becomes a broadcast to the manager standing in the corner; members optimize for sounding productive; blockers stay hidden because they sound like failure. The format is innocent; the audience is the bug. Fix the audience (or better, the safety), and any format works.

Effective vs ineffective — two transcripts

Ineffective (status theater)Effective (steering)
  • "Yesterday I worked on the timeline endpoint, today I'll continue, no blockers." (information content: zero)
  • Manager takes notes, asks "when will it be done?" after each answer.
  • Two members silent; one talks 8 of 15 minutes about a framework choice.
  • Board untouched; the "blocked" card from yesterday still sits in In Progress.
  • Meeting ends; three people leave with different beliefs about today's priority.
  • Team walks the board right to left: "What does Done need next for the goal?"
  • "Timeline endpoint is green, but order-status events aren't firing on cancellation — D2, I need 20 min of your pairing after this."
  • "Migration lock still not approved, day 2 — that's now the critical path; SM, can you chase ops today?" (impediment gets an owner and a deadline)
  • Decision in the room: D1+D3 swarm the cancellation bug; invoice report formally dropped — PO is in the room and agrees, board updated on the spot.
  • Ends in 12 minutes; everyone can state the critical path.

Alternatives to the three questions

  • Walk the board right-to-left — focuses on flow toward Done, not on individual activity.
  • Goal check-in: "Are we closer to the Sprint Goal than yesterday? What's the single biggest thing in our way?"
  • Blockers-first: start with what's stuck; if nothing is stuck and the goal is safe, end early. Seriously — end early.
  • Async written + live 10-min blocker call for distributed time zones: keep the written one a plan, not a diary.

Managing the hazards of mid-sprint reality

  • Interruptions: a team policy beats heroics — "support rotation is the single door; urgent-but-not-P1 waits 2h; everything else goes to the backlog." Published, so stakeholders know the contract.
  • Blocked work: blocked cards must be visibly blocked (Module 14's board mechanics) with an age counter — a blocked card older than 2 days is a retro item, not a fact of life.
  • Mid-sprint scope changes: legitimate via PO negotiation (Module 12); illegitimate via hallway requests. The Daily Scrum is where drift gets noticed ("wait, why is D4 building that?").
  • Swarming: finishing one thing beats starting four (Little's Law — Module 15). The Daily Scrum is the coordination point where "we three take the cancellation bug together" happens.
iInterview lens — Module 13
"Is the Daily Scrum a status meeting?" (No — a planning event for Developers; if it's status, fix the audience and safety). "What if the team skips it?" (You lose the daily inspect/adapt; the cost is invisible until the goal is missed — then everyone notices). "Can the PO/SM attend?" (They may if they add value; the event remains the Developers'). "How do you handle a team member who rambles?" (Format change to board-walking + offline parking, not public scolding).

Kanban Fundamentals

Visualize the work, limit WIP, manage flow — evolution, not revolution

Kanban (看板, "signboard") is a method for managing and improving work systems, adapted for knowledge work by David J. Anderson from Toyota's pull system (itself influenced by Taiichi Ohno's production ideas). Its defining stance: start with what you do now, and evolve incrementally — no prescribed roles, events or reorganization. Where Scrum redesigns the container (sprints), Kanban instruments the flow you already have.

Principles and practices

Principle (change management)Core practice (the board system)
  • Start with what you do now — no greenfield redesign
  • Agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change — small experiments, not big bangs
  • Encourage acts of leadership at all levels — anyone may propose an improvement
  • Visualize the workflow (the board)
  • Limit work in progress (the discipline that makes queues visible)
  • Manage flow (metrics: Module 15)
  • Make policies explicit (written rules of pull, done, priority)
  • Implement feedback loops (cadences: daily replenishment, delivery review, ops review)
  • Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally (models + data)

The board: making invisible work arguable

A Kanban board maps the actual states work passes through, including the queues — and the queues are the point: "In Progress → Code Review" hides a waiting line; "In Progress → Waiting for Review → Reviewing" shows it. Cards carry type, age, owner and blocker state. Simple engineering board:

An advanced board adds: an expedite lane (top swimlane, WIP 1, for genuine production emergencies — its usage rate is a health metric), swimlanes by class of service or work type (features / bugs / incidents / debt), an arrival column mirroring intake, and per-column explicit pull policies ("a card enters Testing only with green CI and a review approval").

Definition of Workflow

The modern Kanban equivalent of Scrum's DoD: a shared, explicit definition of the states, the pull criteria between them, the WIP limits, and what Done means. "Our workflow" stops being tribal memory and becomes inspectable — you can argue about it, measure it, change it deliberately.

WIP limits — the counterintuitive core

  • Pull, not push: a Developer pulls a card when capacity frees, respecting the limit — work cannot be shoved into a full column. This converts "management assigns" into "system admits."
  • Why limiting helps delivery speed: queues grow invisibly, cycle time balloons (Little's Law, Module 15), context switching taxes every item, and blocked items hide behind busy ones. A full column is a visible, early problem: it forces "finish before start," swarming, and the conversation "should we raise the limit, or fix why this stage is slow?"
  • Where to set limits initially: a bit below current average occupancy ("pain now, gain soon"); tune with data, not folklore.
  • Blockers: a card that cannot advance gets flagged blocked with a reason and an age — blocked time is waste made visible; review blockers explicitly in the daily flow meeting.

Work item types and classes of service

ExpediteWIP 1 lane, drop everything, all handsP1 production incident

Classes of service are policies about risk, and they are only real if written next to the board. A team with an expedite lane used weekly has a production-quality problem, not a Kanban problem.

Kanban cadences (the feedback loops)

  • Daily flow stand-up: walk the board right-to-left, focus on aging and blocked items — not personal status.
  • Replenishment (1–2×/week): intake meeting pulls committed work into Ready.
  • Delivery review (biweekly): what shipped, outcomes, stakeholder-facing.
  • Operations review (monthly): capacity vs demand, expedite rate, escalation health.
  • Service delivery review / risk review: metrics against promises (Module 15's percentiles).

Cycle vs lead trips everyone: cycle starts when work starts; lead starts when the request arrives. Lead = queue + cycle. Improving lead time by attacking the queue (triage speed, Ready policies) is often cheaper than making people code faster.

Little's Law

WIP = Throughput × Cycle Time

From queueing theory (John Little, 1961): in a stable system, average WIP equals average throughput multiplied by average cycle time — with units that must be consistent (items = items/week × weeks). Rearranged, it is the flow equation of Kanban: holding throughput steady, raising WIP raises cycle time (things wait longer); cutting WIP at fixed throughput forces cycle time down. That is the mathematical skeleton behind "stop starting, start finishing."

!Assumptions behind the law — say them in interviews
Little's Law describes averages in a steady-state system: arrival ≈ departure, items neither vanish nor duplicate, WIP measured consistently. It is a reasoning tool, not a stopwatch: it will not tell you when a specific item ships. Teams quoting "cycle time = WIP/throughput, therefore your card ships Thursday" are misusing a law of averages as an oracle. Use it to argue about system design (limits, batch sizes, queue placement), and use the cycle-time distribution (below) for forecasts.

Why high WIP hurts (the mechanisms, not slogans)

  • Queueing: every item you start but don't finish sits in someone's (or some column's) queue; queues grow superlinearly with utilization — at ~90%+ utilization, wait times explode (Kingman's formula territory).
  • Context switching: each parallel item costs attention re-loading; 5 parallel items is not 5× throughput/5, it's switch-tax on all five.
  • Delayed integration: long-lived branches + high WIP = merge hell and late risk discovery.
  • Invisibility: blocked work hides behind busy-looking work; WIP limits make "stuck" impossible to not-see.

Forecasting with percentiles

Collect the cycle-time distribution of completed items (scatterplot, x = finish date, y = cycle time; horizontal percentile lines at 50/85/95%). If 85% of historical items finished within 8 days, then "85% of similar new items finish within 8 days" is an honest service-level statement. Monte Carlo forecasting extends this: "what date gives 85% confidence of finishing these 20 items?" from resampled history. This is how Kanban teams answer "when?" without estimating a single story — and the answer always arrives as a probability, because that's what reality sells.

Evolving from one to another

  1. Scrum → Kanban: keep the events first; visualize the true workflow (including queues); add WIP limits softly (columns first, then numbers); let the sprint boundary fade last. Teams that delete the sprint on day one lose their rhythm before the flow system exists.
  2. Kanban → Scrum: the reverse — introduce a cadence (review every 2 weeks), then a goal-shaped planning conversation, then the full container if the work shape supports it.
  3. Any → better: the honest sequence is always visualize → measure → stabilize → improve. Framework labels are packaging; the mechanisms (small batches, limits, feedback loops) are the medicine.
!No framework is universally superior
Anyone selling "Kanban beats Scrum" (or the reverse) is selling their hammer. Fit follows the work's arrival pattern, the team's stability, the organization's ability to absorb change, and the predictability contract stakeholders actually need. Mature engineers can operate both and switch deliberately.

Agile Engineering Practices

Trunk-based development, CI/CD, TDD, refactoring, feature flags — Java / Spring Boot / PostgreSQL / GitHub Actions

⚠The one-sentence thesis of this module
Agile ceremonies cannot compensate for poor engineering quality. Short cycles without automated tests, continuous integration and refactoring don't produce agility — they produce slow regression toward brittle software delivered in smaller pieces. Process speed is bounded by code health; this module is the code-health half of the guide.

The unit of agility is the small, integrated change. Every practice below exists to make merging small changes continuously safe.

Pull requests and code review at speed

  • PR ≤ ~400 changed lines; description answers why + how to test; linked story in the description.
  • CI is the entry ticket: no human reviews what a machine can check (build, tests, lint, coverage delta).
  • Review SLA (Module 15's queue math applies): review work waits in a queue like any other; a 1-working-day SLA keeps the queue short.
  • Squash-merge keeps main's history readable: one story = one commit, bisectable.

Continuous Integration / Delivery / Deployment — the distinctions

  • CI: every push builds + runs the full automated suite against a mainline-integrated state; broken main is a drop-everything event.
  • Continuous Delivery: every green build produces a release candidate that could go to production; deployment is a one-button business decision.
  • Continuous Deployment: green builds go to production automatically; the button is removed. Requires mature monitoring + rollback + flags.

TDD, refactoring, collective ownership

  • TDD (red-green-refactor): write a failing test that states the behavior, make it pass minimally, refactor with the net down. Its Agile value is not coverage dogma — it is that reversibility (safe change) is the engineering precondition for "responding to change." XP's practices were designed as one system; TDD is its load-bearing wall.
  • Refactoring: continuous structural improvement without behavior change, in every PR (the boy-scout rule), plus scheduled debt items for the big moves. A codebase that only ever gains features calcifies; velocity decays invisibly (Module 21's "technical debt ignored" anti-pattern).
  • Collective ownership + coding standards: anyone may improve anything; the formatter (Spotless/Checkstyle) ends the style debates so reviews can argue about things that matter.
  • Pair / mob programming: highest-bandwidth review there is; strongest on risky code, unfamiliar territory, and knowledge transfer — not a lifestyle requirement.

  • Decouple deploy from release: dark-launch the new ETA engine behind tracking.eta.v2; ramp 1% → 10% → 100% while watching error budgets; kill-switch is a config flip, not a rollback.

DORA vs Scrum-specific metrics: velocity and burndown describe a team's internal planning loop; DORA measures the delivery system's external performance — and DORA's advantage is comparability and a research base. The two families answer different questions; healthy organizations use both, at the right altitude (team-local for planning, organization-wide for capability trends).

!Goodhart's Law — the physics of metric corruption
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Make velocity a target → points inflate. Make MTTR a target → incidents get under-reported or "restored" cosmetically while still broken. Make throughput a target → stories get split into confetti. Metrics are thermometers: useful precisely because nobody bribes a thermometer. Guard rails: never attach individual consequences; pair every target-shaped metric with a counter-metric (speed with change-failure rate); prefer trends over snapshots; and let the people being measured see the same dashboards.

Escaped defects and predictability

  • Escaped defects (found in production, per release or per period) measure the gap between your DoD and reality. A rising escape rate with a "green" CI suite means tests are confirming what the code does, not what it must do.
  • Predictability (say/do ratio — delivered vs committed scope) should be read as a forecast calibration metric, not a virtue test. Persistent 60%? Either planning lies or reality interrupts — both are fixable system problems, not character problems.

What each metric cannot tell you

  • Velocity: nothing about value, quality or cross-team comparability.
  • Throughput: counts items, not importance — a spike, a P3 bug and a flagship feature all count as 1.
  • Cycle time: nothing about whether waiting time was well-spent (a deliberate design review is a queue too).
  • Lead time: includes intake delay — a team can look slow because their intake queue is disresourced.
  • MTTR: says nothing about incident frequency — pair with change failure rate.
  • All of them: nothing about whether the product is getting better for users. That needs outcome metrics (activation, retention, support-ticket volume, revenue) — measure activity to improve the system, outcomes to check the direction.

Dashboard hygiene — an interpretation drill

  • Drill 1: Burndown flat-lining at 40% remaining on day 8 of 10, board shows 6 items In Progress. → Classic WIP congestion, not laziness: swarm to finish (Module 13/15); investigate why everything started and nothing finished (review queue? blocked dependency?).
  • Drill 2: Deploy frequency daily, but change failure rate climbed 5% → 18% over two months. → Speed outran the safety net: check test depth on changed paths, flag hygiene, review load. The pairing rule (speed + failure) just did its job.
  • Drill 3: Throughput steady 10/wk; escaped defects zero; but support tickets about "finding things" up 40%. → Feature factory signature: the work flowing is not the work needed — a product-discovery problem, invisible to every flow chart. This is why outcome metrics close the loop.
⚠Never rank individuals by delivery metrics
Individual velocity rankings, personal cycle-time leagues, "top committer" boards — all of them destroy the collaboration these systems need, all of them are statistically meaningless (work assignment is not random), and all of them teach people to optimize the measurement instead of the outcome. The correct object of measurement is the system. When a stakeholder asks "who is slow?", reframe: "which queue is slow, and what made it that way?"

Agile Risk, Dependencies, and Change Management

Risk matrices, dependency mapping, spikes, and the production bug at 2pm on Wednesday

Risk management, Agile-style

Waterfall managed risk with a register reviewed at gates; Agile manages risk structurally — short cycles shrink the batch at risk, working software surfaces technical risk early, and ordering attacks the riskiest-highest-value work first ("fail fast" is a risk strategy, not a slogan). The register still exists, just living in the backlog and the sprint plan.

L = likelihood, I = impact. The matrix (probability × impact grid) is a communication tool: it makes the top-right corner undeniable in a planning conversation. Its honesty depends on reassessment cadence — stale matrices are worse than none.

Dependency mapping

  • Internal dependencies (story A needs story B): dissolve by splitting and re-ordering; if unavoidable, sequence explicitly at planning (Module 12, step 8).
  • Cross-team dependencies (API from the platform team): make them visible artifacts — named stories in both backlogs, a contract (OpenAPI/Pact) as the interface, and a date that appears in both teams' plans. Module 20 scales this.
  • External dependencies (PSP, telecom SMS gateway, ops approvals): treat as third-party queues — they obey Module 15's queueing physics; add explicit waiting columns and escalate on aging, not on anxiety.
  • Technical uncertainty (we don't know if X is possible): spike it, time-boxed, with a written question it must answer — "can MQTT sustain 10k concurrent clients on one broker with p95 < 200ms?" A spike without a question is exploration tourism.

Scope and priority changes

  • Within a sprint: PO negotiates a trade, never an addition (Module 12). The change either replaces something or it explicitly renegotiates the goal.
  • Between sprints: normal — the backlog re-orders. If re-ordering is constant and violent, the real problem is upstream (strategy churn, sales-driven scope) — surface it with data: how much started work was deprioritized mid-flight this quarter?
  • Production incidents mid-sprint: an incident is unplanned work with a class of service (expedite). It consumes capacity visibly (board!), displaces something visibly, and feeds the retro — not silently, not heroically.
  • Urgent bugs: triage by cost of delay, not by who shouts loudest (Module 06). P1s go to the expedite lane; P3s go to the backlog like citizens.
  • Security vulnerabilities: fixed as fixed-date class work with SLAs (e.g., critical ≤ 7 days); the backlog's security items carry their CVE reference and exploitability note.
⚠Scenario — the critical bug at mid-sprint, without the process checklist
Wednesday 14:00, sprint day 7 of 10: customers see wrong delivery addresses on ~4% of orders. Triage: data-corruption class, not cosmetic — customer trust, maybe legal. First move is understanding, not action: D3 and D4 (context on the address flow) reproduce it while the PO and support draft customer comms. Root cause by 15:40: the timezone-patch from sprint 7 writes pickup_window_start in UTC where the address normalizer expects local time — a boundary the happy-path tests never crossed (edge cases, Module 07). Now the decision, made together: fix forward (small, understood) over revert (touches 6 migrated stories' data); tracking story (goal-critical) loses D1’s Thursday to the fix; the invoice report — already the named sacrifice — formally leaves the sprint with the PO's public assent; support gets the comms template and the affected-order list (SQL written by D3). Friday hotfix ships behind a flag at 17:10, corrupt rows repaired by migration script V9 with a dry-run report. Retro adds two items, not one: the missing boundary test and the planning smell that a "small patch" needed no test review. What made this Agile rather than heroic: visibility (board showed the swap), joint decision (PO in the room), sacrifice list pre-agreed, DoD held (the fix got tests + migration discipline), and the incident became system learning instead of blame. The Delivery Simulation lets you make these calls yourself and live with the consequences.

Stakeholder communication during churn

  • Bad news travels at the speed of the Daily Scrum, not the Review: say "goal at risk" on the day it becomes true, with the current best plan.
  • Forecast conversations quote ranges and confidence (Module 11) — "mid-sprint disruption moved the 85% date out ~1 sprint" is information; "we're slightly behind" is fog.
  • Release risks get named owners: rollback owner, comms owner, on-call briefing — writing them down is what makes them real at 2am.

Agile at Scale and Distributed Teams

Multi-team coordination, Nexus, LeSS, SAFe, Scrums of Scrums — and the remote reality

Scaling problems arrive when one backlog, one codebase or one mission must flow through several teams. The honest first answer is architectural, not ceremonial: the best coordination mechanism ever invented is a well-defined API boundary (Conway's law runs both ways — restructure the code to restructure the communication). Process frameworks are the second answer, and they all carry a price tag proportional to their ceremony.

Coordination mechanisms, lightest first

  • Shared Product Backlog, one PO: multiple teams pull from one ordered list; a "backlog owner" is not needed — the ordering is the coordination.
  • Scrum of Scrums: post-Daily sync of one delegate per team, a few times a week, strictly about cross-team flow (dependencies, blocks, contracts). Decays fast when it becomes a second status meeting upward.
  • Communities of practice: guild/channel structures for craft alignment (all backend leads, all QA) — alignment without reporting lines.
  • Integration events: joint refinement on contract-heavy stories; API design reviews at boundaries.

!More ceremonies do not solve organizational coordination problems
If dependencies hurt because the architecture couples everything to everything, PI planning will schedule the pain more politely, not remove it. Remote and distributed Agile

  • Async-first, sync-for-decisions: writing (ADRs, RFCs, story docs, recorded demos) becomes the durable medium; synchronous time is reserved for decisions, conflict and design argument. The Review splits into an always-available recorded demo + a decision call.
  • Time-zone spread: overlap hours are a scarce resource — spend them on ceremonies that need interactivity (planning, retro), not on broadcasts (status). Rotating meeting times shares the pain or exposes its injustice, depending on honesty.
  • Distributed sprint planning: pre-reads (capacity math, candidate stories, spike results) before the call; the call decides, it doesn't discover. Board-as-brain applies double.
  • Documentation and communication: a remote team's onboarding doc, ADR trail and runbooks are its osmotic communication. Underinvestment shows up six months later as "why didn't we know this?"
  • Trust signals replace hallway signals: outcomes and flow metrics over visible typing; managers must learn to read work, not attendance.

Organizational impediments — the Scrum Master's real portfolio

  • Budgeting annually while iterating quarterly — the finance model fights the backlog.
  • Incentivizing utilization (everyone 100% busy) — queues guaranteed (Module 15).
  • Component teams where feature teams are needed — every story becomes a relay race.
  • Separate test orgs and release boards — the DoD is organizationally forbidden.
  • Escalating these is slow, political, unglamorous — and it is the highest-leverage Agile work there is. Nobody's retro retro fixes a funding model.

Real-World Case Studies

Five full narratives with artifacts, decisions, trade-offs and lessons

Case A — Building the Real-Time Logistics Platform (Scrum, product discovery)

Context: 6-person backend team (Java/Spring Boot, PostgreSQL, Kafka), one PO, one part-time SM, greenfield product. Vision: mid-market shippers get dispatcher-grade visibility without dispatcher-grade cost.

  • Sprints 1–4 (walking skeleton): goal-shaped sprints — "order in, order visible" → "driver assigned" → "status live" → "first paying pilot customer." CI pipeline + Flyway + Testcontainers from sprint 1 (Module 17 paid for itself by sprint 3's migration scare being a 20-minute fix).
  • Estimation: poker for stories, t-shirts for epics; velocity stabilized 26–34 by sprint 6; the team published a range, and sales learned to quote "pilot in 6–8 weeks, 85% confidence."
  • The hard sprint: sprint 12, the tracking p95 broke 2s under pilot load. Sprint Goal rewritten mid-flight from "ETA predictions" to "tracking p95 < 2s at 10k concurrent" — the PO traded scope openly, retro flagged the missing load test in DoD; DoD amended (non-functional AC now standard, Module 07).
  • Review that changed the product: pilot dispatchers ignored the "beautiful" dashboard and lived in the exceptions list → backlog re-ordered around exception-first workflow. Usage instrumentation (outcome metrics) caught what opinions missed.
  • Metrics after 6 months: deploy frequency ~daily, lead time 2.1 days, CFR 8%, MTTR 50 min; velocity band stable; churn at pilot accounts down 31%.
  • Trade-offs made: auto-assign algorithm deferred 3 sprints for tracking reliability (right call: churn data); report engine bought (vendor) instead of built — WSJF said build-cost > CoL.
  • Lessons: Sprint Goals carried the team through two crises without heroics; test-first on migrations converted the scariest work into routine; the range-based forecast survived contact with sales.

Case B — Production Support Team (Kanban, unpredictable intake)

Context: 5 engineers owning payments + integrations across 3 products; intake = incidents (unpredictable), partner requests (deadline-y), and a debt backlog nobody looked at. Scrum failed twice ("every sprint failed").

  • The switch: Kanban with an honest workflow: New → Triage → Ready → In Progress (WIP 3) → Review (WIP 2) → Verify → Done, plus an expedite lane (WIP 1). Classes of service: expedite / fixed-date (partner cutoffs) / standard / intangible (debt).
  • Work intake: triage daily 15-min with the on-call + a product proxy; everything gets a class, an owner-or-queue decision, and a cost-of-delay-ish sort — nothing enters without leaving triage.
  • First months: cycle time data revealed the Review queue as bottleneck (median 1.9d waiting) → review SLA + rotating "reviewer of the day"; flow efficiency was 22% — most "cycle time" was waiting, which reframed every conversation about "slow engineers."
  • Forecasting: percentiles from 4 months of history: 50% = 3.1d, 85% = 8.5d. Partner asks "when can you do the webhook retry fix?" → "85% within 9 working days from start" — and they started believing it because it kept being true.
  • Expedite audit: expedite lane usage fell from 38% to 11% of items once the top recurring incident (a flaky PSP webhook receiver) got a real fix — the lane data itself became the argument for root-cause work.
  • Lessons: predictability came from statistics, not promises; WIP limits converted "we're drowning" into "column 4 is full — fix column 4"; the debt (intangible) class got a protected weekly slot or it would never have happened.

Case C — Legacy System Modernization (incremental, risk-led)

Context: 8 engineers; 900k-line monolith (10 years old) running core freight billing; monthly release train; 6% of the codebase touched by any automated test.

  • The plan was a backlog, not a program: debt items ordered by blast-radius × change-frequency (a risk matrix, Module 19, applied to code): the order-pricing module (changed constantly, breaks expensively) went first; the reporting module (static, safe) last — "rewrite the scary hot spots, strangle the boring edges."
  • Characterization tests: before touching pricing, the team recorded real production inputs (anonymized) and pinned current behavior with golden-master tests — not to bless the behavior, but to make change observable. Where behavior was insane (three rounding modes coexisting), tests documented the insanity, PO decided which mode was "true."
  • Strangler pattern: new pricing service behind a flag at the call boundary; dual-run for a month comparing outputs on 100% of traffic (differences → bug queue); cutover by customer segment. Zero big-bang nights.
  • Database: expand-contract migrations (Module 17); every contract step sized to a release window with a measured lock budget and a rehearsed rollback.
  • Stakeholder feedback: each migrated slice demoed with a "before/after invoice" comparison — finance signed off per slice, not per project; the modernization survived two reorgs because value shipped continuously.
  • 18 months later: pricing + address + dispatch migrated; test coverage 6% → 54% on migrated paths; monthly train → weekly deploys on the strangler boundary; incident rate on migrated modules down 70%.
  • Lessons: modernization is a product with a backlog and releases, not a project with an end date; characterization tests are the cheapest courage money can buy; risk-ordering (not architectural elegance) decided the sequence.

Case D — Distributed Engineering Team (remote-first Agile)

Context: 3 sub-teams across Berlin, Bengaluru and São Paulo (UTC-3/IST/CEST) on one platform product; 4–5 overlap hours between extremes; history of "us vs them" handoff politics.

  • Async-first design: decisions by written RFC with a 48-hour comment window (time zones fair by construction); ADRs mandatory for architecture; planning pre-reads 24h before the call. The planning call dropped from 3h to 70 minutes.
  • Cadence with rotation: the one all-hands ceremony (review) rotated its time slot monthly — pain shared deliberately; dailies were per-team + a 15-min cross-team "board walk" recorded for the other side to watch with morning coffee.
  • Dependencies: cross-timezone work (Berlin API consumed by Bengaluru service) got contract-first development: OpenAPI + Pact, agreed in a joint session, then asynchronous — the contract became the colleague awake in your timezone.
  • Onboarding & docs as osmotic communication: a "first week of truth" doc standard; every service README answered the same five questions; tribal knowledge decay measurably (onboarding time 6 weeks → 2.5).
  • Failures en route: a 6-month experiment with a "follow-the-sun" handoff of in-progress stories failed (context loss ate the gains) — killed by the retro with data; a chat-only retro produced 3 words per person until they added silent-writing first.
  • Lessons: writing is the remote team's air; contracts and docs are the substitutes for hallway proximity; fairness mechanics (rotation, async windows) must be explicit or politics fills the gap.

Case E — Failed Sprint Recovery (diagnosis and turnaround)

Context: 7-person team, 10 sprints, goal success 3/10, carry-over average 35%, velocity "unstable" (real answer: declining), retro action list of 40 items, morale bleeding.

  1. Diagnosis (data, not vibes): carry-over analysis — 71% of carried stories were blocked on one external dependency (security review queue, median wait 6.5 days); unplanned intake averaged 34% of capacity (hallway requests); DoD quietly eroded (tests "deferred" to survive sprints); planning selected to 120% of best-ever velocity.
  2. The intervention stack (one change per sprint, owned): sprint 11 — WIP discipline + block-first dailies; sprint 12 — intake policy ("single door = PO; P1s only through expedite") with the visible unplanned-work column; sprint 13 — dependency SLA negotiated with security (48h review SLA, batching PRs smaller); sprint 14 — planning to the demonstrated 24–31 range with a named sacrifice list; sprint 15 — DoD restored (the deferred tests became a visible debt spike, paid once).
  3. The politics: the hallway-request pipeline belonged to a director; the PO carried the data (34% unplanned, 3/10 goals) to a steering meeting and got the intake policy backed — numbers did what charm couldn't.
  4. Results after 6 sprints: goal success 8/10... then a 9th missed — a genuine market shock, not system failure, and the team knew the difference, which is what a healthy system feels like: failures become information, not identity. Carry-over 8%; velocity band honest; unplanned intake 9%.
  5. Lessons: "unstable velocity" was four different solvable problems wearing one trench coat; every fix was a system fix (policy, queue, planning arithmetic), not a people fix; and the retro list shrank to 4 items because the system stopped generating dysfunction faster than they could list it.
